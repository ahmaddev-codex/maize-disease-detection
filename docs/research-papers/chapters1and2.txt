CHAPTER ONE: INTRODUCTION 
1.1 Background of the Study 
Agriculture remains one of the most important sectors in the world, especially in developing countries like Nigeria, where many families depend directly on farming for survival. As the country's population continues to grow, the demand for food is increasing rapidly. Farmers are expected to produce more crops, yet they face serious challenges such as climate change, irregular rainfall, soil degradation, and the spread of plant diseases. These problems make food production more difficult and increase the risk of crop losses.
Maize(Zea mays) is one of the most important crops grown worldwide. It serves as food for millions of humans, feed for animals, and raw material for numerous agricultural industries. In many African countries, maize is a staple food and a major source of income for small farmers. Nigeria alone ranks one of the top ten maize producers globally, with annual production exceeding 11 million metric tons (Food and Agriculture Organization, 2024).
Despite the huge production capacity, maize plants are highly vulnerable to diseases which affect their leaves, stems, and reduce their nutrients. When diseases such as Northern Corn Leaf Blight (Exserohilum turcicum), Common Rust (Puccinia sorghi), Gray Leaf Spot (Cercospora zeae-maydis), and Maize Streak Virus attack maize crops, they can cause devastating yield losses estimated between 20% and 40% of the annual crop loss in Nigeria (Adedeji et al., 2023). 
These diseases spread quickly across the field, and if they are not detected early, they significantly reduce crop production, which will cause serious financial losses for farmers. Studies have shown that plant diseases contribute to major global crop losses every year, affecting food security and farmer’s livelihoods (Upadhyay, Chandel et al., 2025).
For many years, detecting maize diseases has majorly been done by manual inspection. Farmers physically visit their farms, observe the crop leaves, identify diseases based on visible symptoms, and recommend appropriate interventions. While this method is simple and has been in use for decades, it has many weaknesses. 
It takes time and effort, especially on large farms. 
It depends heavily on the farmer's experience. 
Two people may interpret the same symptoms differently.
Early-stage infections are often too subtle to notice (Mamat, Othman et al., 2022).
In rural areas, there is often a shortage of trained agricultural experts, which makes accurate diagnosis even more difficult (Anwar Omer et al., 2024). 
Due to these limitations, diseases are sometimes detected too late, after serious damage has already been done to the crops. This has created an urgent need for alternative approaches that can provide accurate, timely, and accessible disease diagnosis to farmers. This need has intensified in recent years as climate change has altered disease patterns, introduced new pathogens to previously unaffected regions, and created environmental conditions that favor disease proliferation (Ibrahim et al., 2025).
To improve the situation, researchers explored the use of artificial intelligence (AI), particularly deep learning. Deep learning, which is a subset of machine learning, was inspired by the structure and function of the human brain to demonstrate remarkable capabilities in image recognition tasks across diverse domains.
Deep learning models such as Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) can analyze images of plant leaves, stems, or fruits and automatically detect disease patterns because they are built to understand images by passing the images through several layers that gradually learn their features from simple to complex. Unlike traditional computer vision methods that need manual feature selection, CNNs automatically learn useful patterns from the data they are trained on. 
In plant disease detection, CNNs can recognize small signs such as color changes, texture differences, lesion shapes, and other visual symptoms that indicate particular diseases.  Many studies report very high accuracy rates when these models are tested using clean and well-labeled datasets (Ibrahim, Mohsen et al., 2025; Junaidi, Hashim et al., 2025). These systems are faster than human inspection and provide consistent results without stress.
However, there is an important gap. Most existing research was tested under controlled laboratory conditions, forgetting that real farm environments are very different. Things like lighting conditions, leaves overlap, more complex backgrounds, and images captured in the field would likely be different from real farms. 
In addition, many farmers farm in rural areas where resources like internet access, electricity supply and advanced technical devices are unavailable or unreliable. Studies also point out that many AI systems require large datasets and high computational power, which makes them difficult to deploy in rural areas (Upadhyay, Chandel et al., 2025; Omer, Morsey et al., 2024). This means that although technology shows promise, practical real-time deployment in the field will remain a challenge.
With edge computing, practical real-time deployment will no longer be a challenge because it carries out processing directly on devices with limited resources instead of sending data to remote servers. This helps solve the problem of poor internet access because AI systems can run on smartphones, tablets, or low-cost devices like Raspberry Pi without needing connectivity. However, using edge devices also brings challenges such as limited storage, processing power, and battery life. Therefore, building disease detection systems for these devices requires careful design and optimization so they remain accurate while using minimal resources.
Furthermore, many existing systems rely only on visual information from leaf images - known as image data, meanwhile crop disease may be influenced by other factors such as crop variety, planting date, soil conditions, weather patterns, previous disease history, environmental conditions, and farm management practices (Lebrini & Gotor, 2024) although datasets qualities are just as important without this additional information, predictions may lack important context. Integrating different sources of information can improve accuracy and help farmers make better decisions.
Optical Character Recognition (OCR) technology offers a solution to the above challenge by providing a simple way to collect the additional farm information. It can extract text from images of seed labels, fertilizer packages, pesticide instructions, or handwritten farm records. When this extracted metadata is integrated with image-based disease detection, the system can provide more accurate diagnoses and more relevant management recommendations.
This research tackles these connected challenges by developing an integrated system for real-time maize disease detection that combines deep learning-based image analysis with OCR-enhanced metadata extraction. The system is designed to run on low-resource devices such as smartphones and is tested directly on farms to ensure it works in real conditions. The goal is to move beyond theory and provide maize farmers with a practical, affordable, and dependable tool for early disease detection and control.
The system also supports Nigeria’s efforts to modernize agriculture and fits into the idea of precision agriculture, where technology is used to improve farming decisions, efficiency, and sustainability. Early disease detection can help farmers treat crops at the right time, avoid excessive chemical use, reduce losses, and improve both food security and income.


1.2 Problem statement
Maize disease poses a critical threat to food security and agricultural sustainability in Nigeria, causing annual yield losses of 20-40% and resulting in substantial economic damage to small farmers who depend on maize cultivation for their livelihoods especially in developing and limited resource agricultural settings(Upadhyay et al., 2025). Detecting these diseases early is very important because it helps farmers take timely action and reduce crop damage (Ibrahim et al., 2025).
However, in many farming communities, identifying diseases still depends on physical inspection by farmers. This method is slow, labor-intensive, and often inaccurate, particularly when diseases are at an early stage (Mamat et al., 2022). It presents word several fundamental problems:
Subjective and Inconsistent Diagnosis
Identifying maize diseases just by looking at the plant is often difficult and not always accurate because it depends on how much knowledge and experience the person has. Many maize diseases appear very similar, showing signs like yellow leaves, spots, or leaf damage, especially at the early stage of infection. This can easily cause confusion and lead to wrong treatment choices. Also, different people may see and interpret the same symptoms in different ways, which makes diagnosis inconsistent. Since most smallholder farmers do not have professional training in plant pathology, correctly identifying maize diseases continues to be a major challenge (Mamat et al., 2022).
Limited Access to Expertise
Access to professional agricultural support is still limited in Nigeria because there are not enough extension workers to attend to the large number of farmers. In many cases, one extension officer is responsible for thousands of farmers, meaning many maize growers, especially those in rural areas, hardly receive timely expert advice. To get help, farmers may have to travel long distances, wait for scheduled visits, or pay consultation fees. These challenges often cause delays in identifying and managing maize diseases (Omer et al., 2024).
Labor Demands and Scalability Issues
Manually checking crops means farmers have to walk through their fields often and look closely at each plant for signs of disease. This might work for small farms, but it becomes very time-consuming and hard to manage on larger farms. Since farmers can’t always inspect their crops regularly, diseases can grow and spread between checks, making manual detection less effective.
Delayed Intervention
Limited access to expert help, irregular crop checks, and uncertainty in diagnosis often mean that disease management is delayed. By the time symptoms are obvious and properly diagnosed, the infection may already be severe, making treatment harder and more expensive. These delays can lead to bigger crop losses and lower the economic benefits of controlling the disease.
Laboratory-Focused Development
Many present AI systems for detecting maize diseases are trained using images taken in labs, where lighting is good, and backgrounds are simple. While this helps the AI learn better, real farms are very different, lighting changes, leaves can overlap, and backgrounds are messy. Because of this, AI models trained on ideal images may not work well in real farm conditions (Mamat et al., 2022).
Single-Source Data Limitation
Most current maize disease detection systems only use pictures of leaves to identify problems. They ignore other important details like the type of maize, when it was planted, the soil quality, past disease outbreaks, and chemicals applied, which all affect how diseases grow and how to treat them. Since this information is usually kept in paper records, it isn’t used by AI systems, which makes their diagnoses less accurate (Junaidi et al., 2025).
High Resource Requirements
Advanced deep learning models need strong computers, plenty of memory, and a reliable internet connection to work. Rural farmers in Nigeria often don’t have these - they face power outages, slow or no internet, and usually only have basic smartphones. Because of this, AI systems that depend on the cloud or high-end hardware are hard for them to use in real farming situations (Omer et al., 2024).
Limited Real-World Testing
There is a clear gap between how well these systems work in the lab and how they perform on real farms. Many studies test their models using carefully prepared images, but they rarely try them out with farmers in real farm conditions over a longer period. Without this kind of testing, it is hard to know if the systems are reliable or easy for farmers to use in everyday farming (Lebrini & Gotor, 2024).
Dataset Challenges
Most available maize disease datasets have problems like too few images, uneven numbers of examples for different diseases, and coverage from only certain regions. These issues can make AI models learn in a biased way and struggle to work well on different maize types, disease strains, or environments. Because of this, models trained on these datasets may not give reliable results for Nigerian farms (Basu & Narayan, 2025).
Overall, these challenges create a clear gap between the promise of AI-based disease detection and its real use in farming communities. Small maize farmers, who make up the majority of producers, still depend on weak traditional methods while many technological solutions remain difficult to access, insufficiently tested, or unsuitable for their working conditions.
Therefore, there is a need for a practical, field-validated system that can detect maize diseases in real time, operate effectively in limited resource environments, and integrate OCR-enhanced metadata to improve reliability and usability. Addressing these gaps will contribute to developing a more accessible and intelligent disease detection solution for maize farmers.


1.3 Aim and Objectives of the Study
Aim
The aim of this study is to develop a Real-time Maize Disease Detection System That Uses Deep Learning Techniques for Accurate Image-Based Diagnosis and Integrates Optical Character Recognition (OCR) to Extract and Combine Useful Metadata From Farm-Related Documents. The system is designed to operate effectively in limited resource agricultural settings, providing farmers with a practical, accessible, and reliable tool for early disease detection and improved decision-making.
Objectives 
To develop a deep learning-based model for accuracy in maize disease detection: The objective is to create a system that can identify maize diseases from leaf images with high accuracy. Unlike earlier systems that work well only under ideal conditions, this model will be designed to handle real farm environment challenges such as variable lighting, overlapping leaves, and complex backgrounds. This ensures the system remains reliable and effective in practical farming environments.
To integrate Optical Character Recognition (OCR) for extracting farm-related information: This objective focuses on collecting additional data from documents like seed labels, fertilizer instructions, pesticide packaging, or handwritten farm records. By combining this information with image-based detection, the system will provide a more complete understanding of the farm situation, leading to better disease identification and actionable advice. This makes the system more comprehensive and useful compared to solutions that rely solely on leaf images.
To design a real-time system suitable for low-resource agricultural environments: The project aims to develop a system that can operate effectively on affordable devices like smartphones and does not require high-speed internet or advanced computing hardware. This ensures that farmers in rural or limited resource areas can access timely and practical disease detection systems, making the system more usable and accessible than traditional lab-based or resource-heavy approaches.
To validate the system in actual farm conditions, The system will be tested directly on maize farms under real-world conditions, rather than only in controlled environments. This ensures that the system performs well with real crops and field scenarios, giving farmers confidence in its accuracy and reliability. It addresses the common limitation of previous models that were not tested in practical settings.
To provide actionable guidance for farmers based on combined image and metadata analysis: Beyond detecting diseases, the system will offer recommendations for disease management, preventive measures, and improved farm practices. By integrating both visual and textual information, the system will support better decision-making and practical action on the farm, making it more beneficial and farmer-friendly than earlier projects that focused only on disease detection.
1.4 Research Questions
This research seeks to answer the following questions:
1. Can a Convolutional Neural Network achieve 90% or higher accuracy in classifying maize diseases from leaf images captured under real farm conditions?
This question addresses the fundamental capability of deep learning for disease detection in practical agricultural settings, moving beyond laboratory performance to assess real-world applicability.
2. Does the integration of OCR-extracted metadata with image-based analysis improve disease detection accuracy compared to image-only detection?
This question tests the core hypothesis that contextual information enhances diagnostic performance, quantifying the value added by multimodal integration.
3. What metadata types (crop variety, planting date, agrochemical history, etc.) contribute most significantly to improved diagnostic accuracy?
This question provides insight into which contextual factors are most valuable for disease diagnosis, informing system design priorities and data collection strategies.
4. Can the integrated detection system operate in real-time (inference time < 2 seconds) on resource-constrained edge devices suitable for deployment in rural Nigerian agriculture?
This question addresses computational feasibility, determining whether the system can meet the practical requirements of edge deployment without compromising user experience.
5. How does the system's performance in controlled environments compare to its performance under actual field conditions with variable lighting, leaf occlusion, and background complexity?
This question quantifies the performance gap between laboratory and field settings, providing insight into robustness and generalization capabilities.
6. What is the level of agreement between the system's diagnoses and expert diagnoses by agricultural extension workers in field validation trials?
This question establishes the system's reliability by comparing automated predictions against human expert judgment, the current gold standard in agricultural practice.
7. What practical challenges affect system usability and farmer acceptance during field deployment?
This question identifies non-technical barriers to adoption, including user interface design, training requirements, and workflow integration challenges.
8. What is the economic cost of implementing the system per farmer, and what is the potential return on investment based on reduced crop losses?
This question addresses economic viability, determining whether the system represents a cost-effective solution that farmers can justify adopting.
These research questions provide specific, measurable criteria against which the success of the research can be evaluated. They span technical performance (questions 1-6) and practical deployment considerations (questions 7-8), ensuring comprehensive assessment of the system's viability as a real-world agricultural tool.

1.7 Definition of Terms
Maize is a cereal crop that is widely grown and consumed all over the world. It serves as food for humans, feed for livestock, and as raw material for various industries. In many countries, maize is a staple food and a major source of income for small-scale farmers. Healthy maize crops are essential for food security and livelihoods.
Plant Disease is any condition that affects the normal growth, health, or productivity of a plant. Diseases can be caused by fungi, bacteria, viruses, or pests. In maize, diseases often damage the leaves, stems, or grains, reducing the plant’s nutrients and overall yield. Detecting diseases early is important to prevent crop losses.
Leaf Blight is a disease that affects maize leaves, causing brown or yellow spots. These spots can grow and cover large areas of the leaf, reducing the plant’s ability to perform photosynthesis. If not treated early, leaf blight can significantly lower the maize yield and affect the farmer’s income.
Rust is a fungal disease that appears as reddish-brown spots on the leaves or stems of maize plants. It weakens the plant by damaging leaf tissue, reducing the plant’s ability to grow and produce grain. Rust can spread quickly across a field, making early detection critical.
Streak virus is a viral disease that creates streak-like patterns on maize leaves. It affects the plant’s nutrient distribution and slows its growth. Infected plants produce fewer grains, leading to lower harvests. Detecting this virus early helps farmers manage the disease before it spreads widely.
Deep learning is a type of artificial intelligence that allows computers to learn patterns from data and make decisions or predictions automatically. In this project, deep learning is used to teach the system to recognize signs of maize diseases from images of leaves.
Convolutional Neural Network (CNN)  is a specific type of deep learning model that works very well with images. It can automatically identify patterns, shapes, and features in pictures. For this project, the CNN will analyze maize leaf images to detect diseases quickly and accurately.
Optical Character Recognition (OCR) is a technology that reads text from images and converts it into digital text. In this project, OCR will be used to extract information from farm documents like seed labels, fertilizer instructions, and handwritten notes, so the system can use this additional data to make better disease predictions.
Metadata is extra information about the crop or farm that can help improve decision-making. Examples include seed type, planting date, fertilizer type, and farm location. By combining metadata with leaf images, the system can give more accurate disease detection and practical recommendations.
Real-Time System provides results immediately after data is entered or captured. In this project, real-time means that a farmer can take a picture of a maize leaf and quickly know if it has a disease, allowing them to take action right away instead of waiting for expert inspection.
Resource-Limited Environment refers to areas where farmers have limited access to electricity, internet, computers, or advanced devices. Designing a system for such conditions means it must work on simple smartphones, offline if needed, and without expensive hardware, making it practical for small-scale farmers.
Field Validation is the process of testing the system directly in real farms instead of in a controlled lab. This ensures the system works under actual conditions like variable lighting, leaf overlap, and natural backgrounds, making it reliable for everyday use by farmers.

CHAPTER TWO
LITERATURE REVIEW
2.1 Introduction
This chapter reviews prior studies on detecting maize diseases using image processing and artificial intelligence techniques. Over the years, many researchers have proposed various methods for identifying maize diseases, especially through the use of deep learning models such as Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs).
This chapter aims to examine these previous works, understand the approaches they used, and highlight their strengths and limitations. Some studies focused mainly on improving accuracy under controlled conditions, while others attempted to develop mobile or real-time systems for practical use. However, certain challenges, such as limited datasets, lack of field testing, and heavy dependence on internet connectivity still remain.
By reviewing these past works, this chapter provides a clearer understanding of the current state of maize disease detection systems. It also helps identify gaps that need improvement, which form the basis for the proposed system in this study.
2.1.1 Project Overview 
Real-time maize disease detection focuses on identifying diseases in maize plants immediately after an image is captured. Instead of waiting for laboratory testing or expert inspection, the system analyzes the image on the spot and provides instant feedback. This is very important in agriculture because plant diseases can spread quickly. The earlier a disease is detected, the easier it is to control and prevent serious crop loss. Delayed detection often leads to reduced yield and financial loss for farmers.
In recent years, deep learning has become one of the most effective approaches for image-based disease detection. Deep learning models, especially Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), are designed to automatically recognize patterns in images. In the case of maize leaves, these patterns may include unusual spots, color changes, wilting, or texture differences that indicate disease. Unlike traditional image processing methods that rely on manually defined features, CNNs learn directly from large datasets of labeled images. This allows them to improve accuracy and adapt to different disease types.
However, many existing systems focus only on analyzing leaf images. While image analysis is powerful, it does not always provide complete information. For example, environmental conditions, farm location, planting history, and treatment records can also influence disease diagnosis and management. This is where metadata becomes important. Metadata refers to additional information related to the crop or farming conditions. When combined with image analysis, metadata can help provide more meaningful and context-aware results.
Optical Character Recognition (OCR) is used in this study as a way of extracting useful information from images or printed materials. OCR technology allows the system to read text from labels, farm records, or other agricultural documents. By integrating OCR with the deep learning model, the system can gather additional details that support better decision-making. Instead of relying only on visual symptoms, the system can combine image data with extracted text information to improve reliability.
The integration of real-time processing, deep learning, and OCR-enhanced metadata aims to create a more practical and complete solution for maize disease detection. The focus is not only on achieving high accuracy in controlled environments but also on ensuring that the system can function effectively in real farming conditions, especially in resource-limited areas. This makes the approach more applicable, accessible, and useful to farmers who need fast and dependable results.
Overall, this study presents a comprehensive approach that combines speed, intelligence, and contextual awareness in maize disease detection.
2.2 Maize Diseases and Their Economic Impact
Maize (Zea mays) is one of the most important staple crops in Nigeria, serving as a major source of food, income, and industrial raw materials. However, maize production is constantly threatened by several diseases that reduce both yield quantity and quality, increase production costs, and pose risks to food security (FAO, 2021; Badu-Apraku & Fakorede, 2017). Understanding these diseases, how they are detected, and their economic consequences is essential for effective control and management.
2.2.1 Common Maize Diseases in Nigeria
A number of maize diseases are commonly observed across Nigerian farming regions:
Northern Corn Leaf Blight (NCLB):
This disease is caused by the fungus Exserohilum turcicum and appears as long, gray-green or tan lesions on maize leaves. It spreads rapidly under conditions of high humidity and moderate temperatures (18–27°C), particularly during the rainy season. In susceptible maize varieties, NCLB can cause yield losses of up to 50% (Badu-Apraku & Fakorede, 2017; Mahlein, 2016).
Common Rust (Puccinia sorghi):
Common rust is characterized by small reddish-brown pustules on leaf surfaces. It develops well in warm and humid environments (20–28°C). Although usually less destructive than NCLB, early infection can reduce photosynthesis and negatively affect grain formation (Patil & Kumar, 2020).
Gray Leaf Spot (GLS):
GLS, caused by Cercospora zeae-maydis, produces narrow, rectangular gray lesions that may merge and lead to premature leaf drying. The disease thrives in areas with heavy rainfall and high humidity, and severe infections can result in yield losses of about 30–40% (Mahlein, 2016; Zhang et al., 2019).
Maize Streak Virus (MSV):
 MSV is a viral disease transmitted by leafhoppers (Cicadulina species). It appears as pale yellow streaks along leaf veins and is particularly severe in young plants. The disease is more common in hot, dry conditions and can cause stunted growth with yield losses ranging from 20–50% (Martin & Shepherd, 2009).
The distribution of these diseases varies across Nigeria. NCLB and GLS are more common in humid southern regions, while MSV is frequently observed in the drier northern savannas. Common rust occurs across most maize-growing areas but tends to be more severe during the rainy season (Badu-Apraku & Fakorede, 2017).
2.2.2 Traditional Disease Detection Methods
In many Nigerian farming communities, maize disease detection relies mainly on traditional approaches, especially visual inspection.
Manual field inspection:
Farmers typically walk through their fields and observe symptoms such as leaf spots, discoloration, or abnormal plant growth. This approach depends heavily on farmers’ experience and may lead to inconsistent identification (Mahlein, 2016).
Role of extension workers:
Agricultural extension officers support farmers by providing guidance on disease identification and management practices. However, limited numbers of extension workers and poor accessibility in rural areas restrict their effectiveness (Aker, 2011).
Limitations and challenges:
Traditional detection methods are time-consuming, labor-intensive, and often inaccurate. Early disease symptoms may be overlooked, and diseases with similar visual signs can be misidentified. These challenges can delay intervention, allowing diseases to spread and cause greater damage (Mahlein, 2016; Zhang et al., 2019).
2.2.3 Economic Impact of Maize Diseases
Maize diseases have notable economic consequences in Nigeria, affecting farmers, markets, and food availability.
Yield losses:
Research indicates that diseases such as NCLB, GLS, and MSV can reduce maize yields by 20–50% when conditions favor disease development. In severe outbreaks, entire fields may be affected (Badu-Apraku & Fakorede, 2017; Mahlein, 2016).
Financial losses:
Reduced yields directly translate to income loss for farmers. At the national level, maize disease outbreaks contribute to substantial economic losses through reduced production and increased spending on control measures such as pesticides, fungicides, and improved seeds (FAO, 2021).
Food security implications:
Because maize is widely consumed in Nigeria, reduced production can affect food availability and affordability. Lower supply may increase market prices, contribute to nutritional challenges, and increase reliance on imports, thereby impacting both household welfare and national food security (FAO, 2021).
Overall, the prevalence of maize diseases, combined with limitations in traditional detection methods and their economic consequences, highlights the importance of timely and accurate disease identification. This need has driven interest in modern approaches such as AI-based disease detection systems, which can support or enhance conventional practices (Zhang et al., 2019).
2.3 Review of Technical Concepts
Deep Learning for Image-Based Disease Detection


Deep learning, particularly Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), is widely used for analyzing plant leaf images.
CNNs automatically extract features such as color patterns, textures, and shapes, which are critical for distinguishing healthy leaves from diseased ones (Upadhyay et al., 2025; Junaidi et al., 2025).
Recent reviews show that deep learning models can achieve high accuracy, but their performance depends on large, diverse datasets and training on real farm images rather than lab conditions (Raju & Thasleema, 2025).


Optical Character Recognition (OCR) for Metadata Extraction


OCR allows systems to read textual information from images, such as farm labels, timestamps, and environmental data.
Integrating OCR metadata with visual analysis improves model decisions, especially when visual symptoms are unclear or partially hidden (Sharma et al., 2024; Katharria et al., 2025).
However, OCR can struggle with blurred, low-resolution, or handwritten labels, which is a common challenge in field conditions.


Metadata-Enhanced Feature Fusion


Combining visual features from deep learning models with structured metadata (like location, humidity, and temperature) is called feature fusion.
Effective fusion improves prediction accuracy, but poorly designed fusion can lead the system to ignore useful data or rely on incorrect metadata (Ibrahim et al., 2025).
Reviews highlight that hybrid approaches combining image and metadata features are increasingly popular in smart agriculture systems.


Real-Time Processing and Edge Deployment


For real-time detection, models must run on mobile or edge devices with limited computational resources.
Reviews emphasize a trade-off between model complexity and processing speed—large CNNs are accurate but slow, whereas lightweight models are faster but may lose accuracy (Junaidi et al., 2025; Ahmad & Nabi, 2021).
Edge deployment is especially important for resource-limited farms where internet access may be unstable.


Field Validation in Real Agricultural Settings


Many studies test models only in controlled lab environments, but field validation is critical for real-world performance.
Field-tested systems must handle variable lighting, occluded leaves, overlapping crops, and environmental noise (Hashem et al., 2024; Hati & Singh, 2021).m 
Reviews highlight that real-world validation is a key step for ensuring reliability and farmer adoption.


Datasets and Evaluation Metrics


Reviews note that publicly available maize disease datasets are often small or unbalanced, affecting model generalization (Basu & Narayan, 2025).


Evaluation commonly uses metrics like accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score, but there is no standard benchmark, making it difficult to compare systems fairly (Orchi et al., 2021; Abdel-Basset et al., 2024).

2.4 Reviews of Articles
Artificial intelligence (AI) has significantly advanced agricultural disease diagnosis, particularly in maize farming.  
2.4.1 Key Findings from 30 Articles on AI in Agriculture
1. High Accuracy in Disease Detection
AI and deep learning models, including CNNs, YOLOv8, ResNet, and Vision Transformers (ViTs), have demonstrated exceptional accuracy in plant disease detection, often exceeding 95% in many studies (Upadhyay et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2025; Ibrahim et al., 2025). The use of multispectral, hyperspectral, and thermal imaging allows these models to detect diseases even before visible symptoms appear, enabling timely interventions (Mamat et al., 2022; Lebrini & Gotor, 2024). Some systems are optimized for edge devices such as Raspberry Pi or Jetson Nano, providing real-time alerts to farmers directly in the field (Ibrahim et al., 2025; Upadhyay et al., 2025).
2. Automation & Efficiency
AI systems automate crop monitoring, reducing reliance on constant human supervision, lowering labor costs, and minimizing errors (Mohyuddin et al., 2024; Aziz et al., 2025). Smart algorithms guide irrigation, pest control, and fertilizer application with precision. Drones and UAVs combined with AI help farmers map fields, detect diseases, and manage crops more efficiently than traditional methods, boosting productivity and reducing losses (Abdel-Basset et al., 2024; Mesías-Ruiz et al., 2023).
3. Data Challenges
A major limitation is the need for large, labeled datasets for effective model training. Many datasets focus on specific crops or regions, limiting generalizability (Ngongoma et al., 2023; Mohyuddin et al., 2024). Researchers address this using transfer learning, data augmentation, and few-shot learning to enhance performance with limited data (Upadhyay et al., 2025; Mamat et al., 2022).
4. Model and Technology Trends
CNNs remain the most widely used architecture for analyzing crop images, while ViTs are gaining popularity due to their ability to capture complex spatial patterns (Upadhyay et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2025). Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) and self-supervised learning help generate additional training data and improve accuracy when labeled datasets are scarce (Upadhyay et al., 2025; Mamat et al., 2022). IoT integration and edge computing are increasingly used for real-time monitoring directly on farms without relying on cloud processing (Ibrahim et al., 2025; Abdel-Basset et al., 2024).
5. Integration of Multiple Technologies
Modern smart agriculture combines AI, IoT, and remote sensing to improve farming decisions. For instance, fusing RGB, thermal, and hyperspectral data provides a comprehensive view of plant health. AI also supports plant phenotyping, yield prediction, and simultaneous detection of multiple diseases (Hati & Singh, 2021; Upadhyay et al., 2025; Mesías-Ruiz et al., 2023). This multi-technology integration enhances farm intelligence and operational efficiency.
6. Real-World Applicability
While AI models perform well in controlled environments, field conditions such as variable sunlight, weather, and overlapping leaves can reduce accuracy (Lebrini & Gotor, 2024; Ngongoma et al., 2023). Lightweight AI models and edge devices improve accessibility for resource-limited farms, allowing farmers to upload images and receive actionable recommendations in real time (Ibrahim et al., 2025; Upadhyay et al., 2025).
7. Sustainability and Environmental Impact
AI contributes to sustainable farming by reducing chemical inputs. Targeted disease and pest detection ensures interventions are applied only where necessary, reducing water, fertilizer, and pesticide use. These technologies help lower agriculture’s environmental footprint while increasing efficiency (Akbar et al., 2024; Upadhyay et al., 2025).
8. Limitations Identified
Despite its promise, AI in agriculture faces challenges:
High computational demands limit adoption by small-scale farms (Mohyuddin et al., 2024).
Many models operate as “black boxes,” reducing interpretability and trust (Upadhyay et al., 2025; Lebrini & Gotor, 2024).
Limited and non-standardized datasets hinder generalizability (Ngongoma et al., 2023; Mamat et al., 2022).
Most research remains at the prototype stage, restricting large-scale deployment under diverse farm conditions (Jiang et al., 2025; Ibrahim et al., 2025).
9. Future Research Directions
Recommended directions include:
Creating diverse, standardized datasets covering multiple crops, regions, and diseases (Upadhyay et al., 2025; Mamat et al., 2022).
Developing lightweight AI models for edge devices suitable for low-resource farms (Ibrahim et al., 2025).
Implementing explainable AI to increase farmer trust and usability (Lebrini & Gotor, 2024).
Integrating AI with IoT and mobile technology for real-time disease monitoring (Abdel-Basset et al., 2024).
Enhancing models to detect multiple diseases and pests simultaneously and perform robustly in real-world conditions (Upadhyay et al., 2025; Mesías-Ruiz et al., 2023).
10. Overall Insight
Across all 30 reviewed studies, AI and deep learning demonstrate transformative potential for agriculture, improving disease detection, yield prediction, pest management, and sustainability. Challenges such as cost, technical complexity, and field robustness remain, but trends in edge computing, multimodal data fusion, and transfer learning are closing the gap between research and practical applications (Upadhyay et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2025; Ibrahim et al., 2025). Smart farming is increasingly becoming data-driven, precise, and environmentally sustainable.

2.5 Current Challenges identified in Literature
Real-time maize disease detection using deep learning combined with OCR-enhanced metadata integration is an exciting advancement in agriculture. These systems can help farmers quickly identify diseases in their crops, which can save time and reduce crop losses. However, despite the promise, there are several challenges that make it difficult to deploy these systems reliably in real farm conditions. These challenges include issues with data, model performance, metadata integration, computing requirements, and practical use in the field.

1. Limited and Imbalanced Datasets
Deep learning models, like Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), need a lot of images to learn how to detect diseases accurately. Unfortunately, many available maize disease datasets are small, private, or collected under lab conditions rather than real farm environments (Junaidi et al., 2025; Upadhyay et al., 2025).
Another problem is imbalance. Some diseases have many images, while others have very few. This means the model learns to recognize common diseases better than rare ones, which can lead to mistakes when detecting less common diseases (Basu & Narayan, 2025).
2. Variability in Field Conditions
Images taken in real farms are often very different from controlled lab images. Lighting changes during the day, leaves may overlap, shadows appear, and backgrounds can be messy. Environmental factors like temperature, humidity, and soil conditions also affect how diseases look (Ahmad & Nabi, 2021; Hati & Singh, 2021). These differences make it harder for models trained on clean images to work well in actual farms.
3. OCR and Metadata Challenges
OCR (Optical Character Recognition) is used to read text information from images, like farm labels, timestamps, or location data. This information can help improve disease detection. But OCR works best on clear images. In real farms, text may be blurry, low-resolution, or written in different fonts, which reduces accuracy (Sharma et al., 2024).
Combining OCR data with visual disease features is also tricky. If done incorrectly, the system might ignore useful metadata or trust wrong information, reducing overall accuracy (Katharria et al., 2025).
4. Real-Time Processing Limitations
Deep learning models can be heavy and require a lot of computing power. Running them on mobile devices or small edge devices in farms is difficult because of limited memory, processor speed, and battery life (Ibrahim et al., 2025; Junaidi et al., 2025). Systems must be fast enough to give farmers immediate feedback while still being accurate.
5. Lack of Standard Evaluation
There is no standard way to test and compare these systems. Different studies use different datasets, metrics, and testing methods (Orchi et al., 2021; Abdel-Basset et al., 2024). This makes it hard to know which model is truly better in real-world farming conditions.
6. Practical Deployment Issues
Even if a model works well, practical issues can prevent its use. Many farmers may not have smartphones with good cameras or stable internet, which limits cloud-based solutions (Ahmad & Nabi, 2021; Hashem et al., 2024). Systems must be easy to use, lightweight, and able to work offline if needed.
7. Dataset Transparency
Many studies focus on the model’s accuracy but do not explain their datasets. Without details about dataset size, diversity, and limitations, it is hard to know if a system will work well in different regions or for different maize varieties (Rani et al., 2023; Akbar et al., 2024).
In summary, while deep learning combined with OCR-enhanced metadata shows great promise for real-time maize disease detection, several challenges remain. Improving dataset availability, handling field variability, ensuring reliable OCR integration, balancing computational demands, standardizing evaluation, and considering practical deployment constraints are all essential to make these systems accurate, reliable, and useful in real agricultural settings.
2.6 Research Gap
Many previous studies have shown that artificial intelligence and deep learning can effectively detect plant diseases from leaf images. Researchers have reported high accuracy when using convolutional neural networks and other advanced models to classify plant diseases, especially when experiments are conducted using well-structured datasets (Mohanty et al., 2016; Ferentinos, 2018; Too et al., 2019). These findings clearly demonstrate that AI has strong potential to support modern agricultural practices and improve disease management.
However, despite these positive results, several important gaps still exist. One major limitation is that many studies depend on publicly available datasets captured under controlled conditions. These datasets often do not reflect real farm environments where variations in lighting, background objects, leaf positioning, and overlapping symptoms are common. As a result, models trained in laboratory settings may struggle to perform consistently in practical field situations (Kamilaris & Prenafeta-Boldú, 2018; Liakos et al., 2018).
Another gap relates to dataset representation and regional relevance. A large portion of existing plant disease datasets focuses on crops or environments outside sub-Saharan Africa. This creates a challenge for developing models that accurately represent maize diseases common in Nigeria, where climatic conditions, disease prevalence, and farming practices differ. The lack of locally representative datasets therefore limits the reliability and adaptability of many proposed solutions.
Deployment challenges also remain insufficiently addressed. While several models achieve strong performance, they often require high computational power and stable internet connectivity through cloud-based processing. Such requirements may not be practical for many rural farming communities where internet access, electricity, and advanced computing resources are limited. This highlights the need for lightweight models that can operate efficiently on mobile or edge devices (Zhang et al., 2019; Chen & Lin, 2020).
In addition, most studies focus on detecting a single disease at a time, whereas farmers frequently encounter multiple diseases or stress conditions simultaneously. This indicates a need for more robust detection systems capable of handling multi-disease scenarios that better reflect real agricultural conditions.
Finally, usability and accessibility considerations are often overlooked. Farmers need solutions that are easy to use, understandable, and capable of providing meaningful guidance rather than only prediction outputs. Without addressing user interaction and practical adoption factors, even highly accurate systems may have limited real-world impact.
Based on these observations, this study aims to contribute by exploring a maize disease detection approach that considers real-field variability, local disease relevance, and deployment on resource-constrained edge devices. By focusing on both technical performance and practical usability, the research seeks to move closer to solutions that can realistically support farmers in everyday agricultural activities.
2.7 Conceptual Positioning of Proposed System

This system uses deep learning to detect diseases on maize leaves in real time. It takes images of the leaves and automatically identifies if a plant is healthy or affected by a specific disease. To improve accuracy, it also uses OCR (Optical Character Recognition) to read metadata, like farm labels, timestamps, or environmental information, and combines this with the visual analysis. The system is tested in real farms and designed to work on low-cost devices, making it practical for farmers in areas with limited resources. This way, farmers get fast, accurate, and accessible disease detection to help protect their crops.
